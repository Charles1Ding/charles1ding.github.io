Substitutional vs Interstitial — different atom sites, same solid-solution strengthening

Pure Metal

Atom layers slide freely like a stack of cards — excellent ductility.

Substitutional Alloy

Solute atoms replace host atoms at lattice sites; size mismatch distorts the lattice and partly blocks slip.

Substitutional Alloy

Mechanism: Solute atoms have a radius close to the host (difference usually < 15%) and directly replace host atoms at normal lattice sites.

Examples: Brass (Cu–Zn), Bronze (Cu–Sn), solder (Sn–Pb).

Strengthening: Size/electronegativity differences elastically distort the nearby lattice and impede dislocation motion → strength↑, ductility still good.

Interstitial Alloy

Mechanism: Solute atoms are very small (e.g. C, N, B); they cannot replace large atoms, so they slip into the interstitial gaps between host atoms.

Examples: Steel (Fe–C), tungsten carbide (W–C). Just ~1/50 carbon atoms can vastly harden iron.

Strengthening: Tiny atoms wedge into the gaps, creating strong local strain fields that "pin" dislocations → hardness↑↑, more brittle.

What is the difference between the two?

Both are forms of solid-solution strengthening, but the atoms "sit" in different places: a substitutional atom takes a normal lattice site (swaps a seat), while an interstitial atom crams into the gap between sites (squeezes into the void). Because interstitial atoms are so small and wedge in so tightly, even a tiny amount produces very strong hardening — that is why a little carbon turns soft wrought iron into hard steel.
